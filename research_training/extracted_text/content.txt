Abstract

This Equity Research report initiates coverage of GreenVolt (GVOLT), which
is a Portugal-based developer and independent power producer operating in the
biomass, solar PV, wind, and distributed energy generation segments. Through
a SOTP, we captured the high visibility and steady cashflows on biomass and
distributed generation via DCF and we crystalized solar PV and wind’s value
via EV/MW, where different technologies and pipeline stages were taken into
consideration. We issue a Buy rating, with a Target Price of €9.50/share,
implying an upside of c.17%, over the next 12 months. Macroeconomic and
industry factors are favourable to GreenVolt’s equity story of becoming a
powerhouse in the renewable energy generation market. Risks to the upside
include in-place biomass remuneration schemes extensions. Risks to the
downside include high mortality rate for early-stage projects and management
dependency.

Introduction
This Equity Research report aims at initiating coverage of GreenVolt, which is a Portugal-based
developer and independent power producer operating in the biomass, solar PV, wind, and
distributed energy generation segments.
To do so, we start by providing a Company overview, where we look into GreenVolt’s business
segments, geographic footprint, value chain, and its recently updated strategy plan for 2022-
2026. It follows a Macroeconomic context, that allows the reader to understand how do recent
worldwide events play a role for a renewable energy generator player like GreenVolt. Then, we
provide an Industry overview, where we assess demand and supply factors driving renewables,
and we present the industry’s revenue and costs drivers. Revenue forecast is based on a top-
down approach, where we evaluate if GreenVolt has the required portfolio to capture market
share (in terms of installed capacity and load factor), if other competitors have the required
portfolio to capture market share, and how is the Company affected by long-term trends (in terms
of average selling price, regulatory frameworks, and other macroeconomic factors). For the costs’
assessment, we forecast feedstock costs, instalment costs, OpEx and CapEx. Then, we analyse
some Risks that can impact GreenVolt. Having all of these in mind, we are then able to present
our Valuation case, based on a Sum-of-the-Parts (SOTP) approach, cross-checking with trading
multiples and precedent transactions. In this section, we also detail the discount rates used. At
the end, this report allows the reader to draw meaningful conclusion on whether to invest or not
in GreenVolt.
This present report covers the following sections: Company overview, Macroeconomic context,
and Industry overview (demand and supply and revenue drivers). These sections allow the reader
to have a deep understanding of the Company and the industry’s fundamentals, on which we
build on our forecasts and subsequent valuation methods later on. The remaining sections are
covered by my Equity Research partner.
This analysis yields a rating of Buy, at a Target Price of €9.50/share, implying an upside of c.17%.
This is cross-checked with trading multiples (€7.76/share) and past transactions (€11.46/share).
We conclude GreenVolt is well poised to crystalize value in the asset rotation strategy in the
near-term, highly enjoying long visibility on biomass assets’ cashflows.

Company overview
GreenVolt – Energias Renováveis (or “the Company”) is a developer and independent power
producer (“IPP”), focused on regulated biomass and expanding its presence into solar PV
and wind through promising pan-European pipeline. In 2021, the Company was rebranded as
GreenVolt and executed an IPO, at a share price of €4.25 and market cap over €500m.
Business segments
GreenVolt’s strategy is based on three business areas, biomass, solar PV and onshore wind,
and distributed energy generation (“DG”), which operate under four brands, in light of the
November 2022 image rebranding (Figure 2).
The Company is currently the leading biomass player in Portugal, where it operates five
biomass plants, which use 100% forestry residues, with a total installed capacity of 101MW. It
benefits from a Feed-in-Tariff (“FiT”)1 regime and long-term supply agreements with Altri,
one of the leading eucalyptus pulp producers in Europe, which contribute to a higher competitive
advantage, when compared to other biomass players. In 2019, GreenVolt made its first scaling-
up move in Europe, by acquiring a 51% stake in the Tilbury biomass plant in the United
Kingdom (“UK”), representing 42MW of installed capacity, being solely sourced by urban waste
wood. The six plants culminate in a total installed capacity of 143MW, which produce, on an
annual basis, over 1,000GWh (Figure 3). As of 2021, this business segment contributed with
c.€131m of revenues (representing 93% of total revenues).
GreenVolt is committed to grow well beyond biomass through other renewable energy sources
(“RES”), as solar PV and wind. In fact, the acquisition of V-Ridium Power Group (“V-Ridium”)
in 2021 has set an ambitious pace for the Company. In Q3 2022, the Company had a portfolio
of c.6.7GW, of which 229MW are in operation or UC, 56MW RtB, c.3.1GW in advance stage,
and c.3.3GW early stage (Figure 4).2 As of 2021, the segment contributed with 1% of total
revenues. The Romanian solar park acquired in the second quarter of 2022 (LIONS Solar PV
plant), for €83m, set the grounds for the first operational solar PV and wind capacity of
GreenVolt, amounting to 45MWp.
Together with the development of these large-scale projects, GreenVolt is dedicated to DG,
focused on self-consumption and energy communities. By September 2022, GreenVolt had
33.5MWp of installed capacity and 112.3MWp of backlog. It contributed with c.€8m of revenues
(6% of total revenues) in 2021.
Geographic footprint
Regarding the biomass segment (Figure 5), GreenVolt is expected to continue to leverage
Portugal and UK’s plants, having been identified potential acquisitions of two power plants in the
UK (c.40MW) in the next five years. In Portugal, not only is a new Mortágua plant with 10MW of
installed capacity expected to become operational in the next years, but the Company is
considering greenfield opportunities up to 20MW as well.3

Value chain
The biomass value chain commences with harvesting resources, intermediate storage, energy
production, and energy distribution.4 GreenVolt positions itself in the production of energy part,
but the overall competitive advantage also arises due to favourable conditions relating to the other
steps of the value chain. For example, GreenVolt has a supply agreement with Altri, which
secures the totality of resources used in the Portuguese biomass plants, and all biomass plants
are established in proximity to its raw material suppliers, which minimises transport and storage
costs (Figure 8).5
The renewable energy value chain begins with the development of projects, ranging from their
design, execution and construction, to the operation of the respective plants (Figure 9). Since the
profitability and the level of investment required are inversely proportional to the stage of maturity
of the project, GreenVolt has preferred to operate mainly in the most upstream part of the value
chain, (project development), and only opportunistically in the construction and operations
stages. Project development, which is less capital intensive, but highly challenging in terms of
know-how and expertise, includes all activities from the design of a project to its authorisation.
Strategy plan (2022e-2026e)
A year after the IPO, and post Altri spin-off, GreenVolt updated its original strategy, shifting its
strategic positioning from biomass to solar PV and wind and DG, in light of growth potential,
although the former is currently still the main driver in terms of cashflow generation.6,7
GreenVolt is focused to continually optimise current biomass plants, only investing in new
ones in an opportunistic manner, implying a lower investment than expected until 2026e, when
compared to the Company’s goals at IPO.

of renewable assets, both translating into an expected higher investment until 2026e, in
comparison to what was defined originally. Regarding the former, GreenVolt aims to fully
develop some projects to be held in its balance sheet (c.20% to 30% of existing pipeline)
(Figure 10). Regarding the latter, and as mentioned previously, GreenVolt pursues the value

Macroeconomic context
Russia’s war on Ukraine, which started earlier this year, has pivoted a global energy crisis, due
to geopolitical volatility and constrained access to natural gas, leading to energy prices
spikes. This, in turn, has stressed the need for energy resilience (in terms of supply and
independence), and is acting as a push for RES.10 Moreover, current market instability, namely
high levels of inflation, has increased the demand for long-term PPAs while shifting
investor’s appetite for projects at a COD stage, which are fully de-risked, as mentioned
previously. Even though this trend seems favourable to GreenVolt’s strategy plan, we do not
discard possible shock waves related to supply chain bottleneck which could downgrade sales in
the asset rotation strategy. As a result, the future seems volatile and hard to predict.

Regarding energy prices, studies have shown, as expected, that the price-elasticity of energy
demand follows the behaviour of microeconomics: higher prices lead to lower consumption
(hence lower demand), and vice-versa.15 Though, the day-ahead wholesale price
determination introduces a peculiar case for renewables. For the determination system in a non-
discriminatory auction (i.e., marginal bid), producers submit supply schedules for each hour of
the day-ahead and power plants are ranked by lowest to highest bid, forming a generation stack.
Then, in a typical generation stack, the baseload plants (i.e., the first dispatched) tend to be
renewables (e.g., hydro, solar, wind), as its marginal costs are virtually zero. Only then do mid-
merit plants and peaking plants play a role (e.g., natural gas, coal), being the last dispatched
producer the one which sets the price, and all winning bidders get paid the same clearing price.
Thus, even if global energy demand fluctuates, renewables are expected to be always consumed
(i.e., demand for renewable energy should not be as impacted). On the other hand (i.e., the supply
side), it is worthwhile to mention the supply concerns that arise from rising levels of
generation from technologies with virtually zero marginal costs, such as renewables, which
will unavoidably reduce prices, such that revenues will not cover investments, and therefore
discourage energy supply from RES. Notwithstanding, we believe the actual pricing system
can be improved, as research as demonstrated (through market or government incentives), so
the supply of energy generated from RES should not be as impacted.16
Although global energy demand may be reaching a plateau level, the three drivers highlighted
above point to a shift in the energy mix towards a greater contribution of renewables, hence
driving renewable energy demand.
In fact, globally, demand for fossil fuels is expected to decline considerably and the global
fuel mix is expected to shift substantially between 2020 and 2050. Coal usage, which reached
its peak in 2014, is bound to decrease by c.15%. Having dropped steeply in 2020 due to the
pandemic, oil demand recovered swiftly, returning to a pre-pandemic level and is expected to
reach a plateau shortly after 2030. Natural gas demand is expected to increase from c.4bn cubic
metres (cm) in 2020 to c.5.7bn cm in 2050. Nuclear energy will expand by 15% between 2020
and 2030, mainly reflecting expansions in China.17 Moreover, the energy sector is expected to
be based mainly on renewable energy: more than half of total energy supply in 2050 stems
from wind, solar, bioenergy, geothermal and hydro energy.17
This positive RES demand outlook has a favourable impact on GreenVolt’s revenues, as
we will further analyse in the industry revenue drivers section.

Solar PV and wind
This segment’s portfolio has 45MW of installed capacity, which pales in comparison with the
c.6.7GW of pipeline. Thus, and since we are not factoring in further acquisitions to current
installed capacity, we will only forecast the current pipeline’s trajectory into installed capacity
per se (i.e., fully operational).
By 2026e, we forecast all current pipeline will become RtB or COD (within the asset rotation
strategy) or operational (within the balance sheet strategy), based on the current Company’s
data on the split between technology, country, and pipeline stage.22 From c.6.7GW of existing
pipeline, we forecast 75% will be under the asset rotation strategy, while the remainder will be
held on the balance sheet.
Regarding the asset rotation scheme, by 2026e, we estimate c.5.4GW will be either at RtB or
COD stages, including the c.1.4GW from storage. As for the assets to be held on the balance
sheet, we forecast the Company will keep assets in countries on which it has more expertise
(i.e., Portugal, Poland, and Greece), and keep the already operational solar PV plant in Romania.
Thus, by 2023e, we expect c.60MW to be held on balance sheet to be fully operational (i.e.,
generating cash inflows), and by 2026e, we expect this number to reach c.1.3GW, thus growing

conclusions regarding GreenVolt market share in this segment.

Load factor
Load factor, also known as capacity factor, offers a glimpse on the reliability of power plants: it
measures how often a plant is running at maximum power and it is given by energy exported
over maximum production possible, as per license.24 This key parameter is crucial to measure
plants’ functioning, and it depends mainly on the technology used, the availability of
resources, and shutdown periods for maintenance. According to IRENA, comparing the three
technologies, biomass has the highest load factor, followed by onshore wind and solar PV.
Notwithstanding, these last two followed an upward trend due to technology advancements, while
biomass followed a trendless period peaking in 2017 with a capacity factor of 87% (Figure 21).25
Biomass
Load factors for biomass plants are usually very high but come at the expense of regular
maintenance work (as boilers often face technical problems), and are highly reliant on continuous
access to biomass supply. GreenVolt’s power plants in Portugal are powered completely by
forestry residues through a long-term agreement by Altri Madeira, and in the UK by urban waste
wood through a long-term contract with Esken Renewables. A clear advantage that contributed
to an overall average load factor improvement over the past years.
In Portugal, in the base scenario, we assume that load factor increases by 7pp until 2026e,
to c.81%, since this amount would have been the average Portuguese load factor as of 2021,
if we excluded the Ródão outage, whose plant load factor would be 71% instead of 40%. In a
bull scenario, we assume load factor increases by 22pp until 2026e, to c.96%, since we
consider GreenVolt will be able to apply its Figueira da Foz II know-how (i.e., use of maximum
quality materials, boiler adjustments, among others) in the remaining Portuguese biomass plants,
and therefore we expect all load factors to converge to Figueira da Foz II current load factor. In
these two scenarios, from 2027e-2044e, load factor remains stable (i.e., growth of 0%). In the
bear scenario, the no-growth assumption (i.e., no improvement) begins at 2022e (Figure 22).
As for the UK load factor (i.e., Tilbury plant), it is already above the national average load factor
(89% vs. 68%, respectively), therefore we do not expect improvement (nor deterioration)
under in any scenario.26
In any case, current load factors (Portugal and UK) are well above the overall worldwide
average in 2021 (68%), which suggests a favourable outlook for GreenVolt’s ability to maintain
its competitive advantage going forward and, as a result, its market share.

Competitive landscape
In light of the Company’s business mix, geographic exposure, and, more importantly, the
stage of pipeline, we believe it is difficult to find a true competitor within the market of energy
generation from RES. Nonetheless, GreenVolt may potentially face competition from several
fronts which can compromise its ability to keep or increase market share.
On one hand, we have traditional oil & gas players (e.g., Repsol, Galp, BP) and integrated
utilities (e.g., Iberdrola, Endesa, EDP, Engie), and on the other, we have renewable players
focused on solar PV and wind (e.g., EDP Renováveis, Acciona, Orsted) and traditional
biomass players (e.g., Albioma and Drax). Since GreenVolt positioning lies in between
traditional biomass players and pure renewable players, it emerges with unique characteristics,

and on developing plants to RtB or COD.

Since there is no historical data on GreenVolt’s ability (or lack thereof) to meet its obligations, we
assume a no-failure case. Thus, the market price is the only relevant driver for the average

crisis. Chris O'Shea, from Centrica, owner of British Gas, said that it is expected that for the next
two years, prices will continue at an all-time high.40 As a result, for all scenarios, in 2023e and
2024e, we considered that the cap will be met on the summer and winter months and, for the
remaining, we used the average spring and autumn months of 2022a (Figure 29). Only in the bull
scenario, we considered that the cap will be extended until 2025e.
From 2027e onwards, we considered a return to pre-Ukraine war electricity price levels, since
current forecasts and research do not suggest otherwise. For the base and bull scenarios, we
assumed the same selling price as for 2022e, which will be indexed to the forecasted inflation for
each year going forward. As for the more severe scenario, we believe selling prices will return to

Company overview
GreenVolt – Energias Renováveis (or “the Company”) is a developer and
independent power producer (“IPP”), focused on regulated biomass and
expanding its presence into solar PV and wind through promising pan-
European pipeline. In 2021, the Company was rebranded as GreenVolt and
executed an IPO, at a share price of €4.25 and market cap over €500m.
Business segments
GreenVolt’s strategy is based on three business areas, biomass, solar PV and
onshore wind, and distributed energy generation (“DG”), which operate under
four brands, in light of the November 2022 image rebranding (Figure 2).
The Company is currently the leading biomass player in Portugal, where it
operates five biomass plants, which use 100% forestry residues, with a total
installed capacity of 101MW. It benefits from a Feed-in-Tariff (“FiT”)1 regime and
long-term supply agreements with Altri, one of the leading eucalyptus pulp
producers in Europe, which contribute to a higher competitive advantage, when
compared to other biomass players. In 2019, GreenVolt made its first scaling-up
move in Europe, by acquiring a 51% stake in the Tilbury biomass plant in the
United Kingdom (“UK”), representing 42MW of installed capacity, being solely
sourced by urban waste wood. The six plants culminate in a total installed
capacity of 143MW, which produce, on an annual basis, over 1,000GWh (Figure
3). As of 2021, this business segment contributed with c.€131m of revenues
(representing 93% of total revenues).
GreenVolt is committed to grow well beyond biomass through other renewable
energy sources (“RES”), as solar PV and wind. In fact, the acquisition of V-
Ridium Power Group (“V-Ridium”) in 2021 has set an ambitious pace for the
Company. In Q3 2022, the Company had a portfolio of c.6.7GW, of which 229MW
are in operation or UC, 56MW RtB, c.3.1GW in advance stage, and c.3.3GW early
stage (Figure 4).2 As of 2021, the segment contributed with 1% of total revenues.
The Romanian solar park acquired in the second quarter of 2022 (LIONS Solar PV
plant), for €83m, set the grounds for the first operational solar PV and wind
capacity of GreenVolt, amounting to 45MWp.

Regarding energy prices, studies have shown, as expected, that the price-elasticity
of energy demand follows the behaviour of microeconomics: higher prices lead
to lower consumption (hence lower demand), and vice-versa.15 Though, the
day-ahead wholesale price determination introduces a peculiar case for
renewables. For the determination system in a non-discriminatory auction (i.e.,
marginal bid), producers submit supply schedules for each hour of the day-ahead
and power plants are ranked by lowest to highest bid, forming a generation stack.
Then, in a typical generation stack, the baseload plants (i.e., the first dispatched)
tend to be renewables (e.g., hydro, solar, wind), as its marginal costs are
virtually zero. Only then do mid-merit plants and peaking plants play a role (e.g.,
natural gas, coal), being the last dispatched producer the one which sets the price,
and all winning bidders get paid the same clearing price. Thus, even if global
energy demand fluctuates, renewables are expected to be always consumed (i.e.,
demand for renewable energy should not be as impacted). On the other hand (i.e.,
the supply side), it is worthwhile to mention the supply concerns that arise from
rising levels of generation from technologies with virtually zero marginal
costs, such as renewables, which will unavoidably reduce prices, such that
revenues will not cover investments, and therefore discourage energy supply
from RES. Notwithstanding, we believe the actual pricing system can be improved,
as research as demonstrated (through market or government incentives), so the
supply of energy generated from RES should not be as impacted.16

▪ Load factor
Load factor, also known as capacity factor, offers a glimpse on the reliability of
power plants: it measures how often a plant is running at maximum power and it is
given by energy exported over maximum production possible, as per
license.24 This key parameter is crucial to measure plants’ functioning, and it
depends mainly on the technology used, the availability of resources, and
shutdown periods for maintenance. According to IRENA, comparing the three
technologies, biomass has the highest load factor, followed by onshore wind
and solar PV. Notwithstanding, these last two followed an upward trend due to
technology advancements, while biomass followed a trendless period peaking in
2017 with a capacity factor of 87% (Figure 21).25

highly reliant on continuous access to biomass supply. GreenVolt’s power plants

agreement by Altri Madeira, and in the UK by urban waste wood through a long-

overall average load factor improvement over the past years.

Solar PV and wind / distributed energy generation
Globally, onshore wind load factor increased 44% from 2010 to 2021. It
depends essentially on two factors: quality and speed of the air, and turbine
technology used. Locations that provide incoming wind consistently, such as
mountainous areas, larger and more reliable turbines, along with higher hub-
heights and larger rotor diameters, increase load factors (Figure 23).25
The global weighted average solar PV capacity factor rose from 14% in 2010
to 17% in 2021. Solar load factor depends essentially on solar resource conditions
and technology used. The upward load factor trend is related to (i.) technology
advancements, such as the adoption of bifacial technology – i.e., power is
produced from both sides of the panel when illuminated, which increases electricity
generation – and the use of trackers – which allow the panel to follow the sun’s
movements and consequently generates more solar power; (ii.) construction of
solar PV in sunnier locations, quantified by the global horizontal irradiation (GHI),
which changes significantly per country (Figure 24); and (iii.) inverter load ratio
(ILR), also known as the DC-to-AC ratio, inverters running close to full capacity
lead to a higher load factor (Figure 25).
Overall, we believe technology will continue to improve, consequently rising
load factors in these two renewable technologies. Although we lack information on
current GreenVolt’s load factor of these two technologies, we believe it is well
positioned to also surf the technological improvements wave, thus maintaining
competitive advantage going forward (and market share, as a consequence).

▪ Competitive landscape
In light of the Company’s business mix, geographic exposure, and, more
importantly, the stage of pipeline, we believe it is difficult to find a true
competitor within the market of energy generation from RES. Nonetheless,
GreenVolt may potentially face competition from several fronts which can
compromise its ability to keep or increase market share.
On one hand, we have traditional oil & gas players (e.g., Repsol, Galp, BP) and
integrated utilities (e.g., Iberdrola, Endesa, EDP, Engie), and on the other, we
have renewable players focused on solar PV and wind (e.g., EDP Renováveis,
Acciona, Orsted) and traditional biomass players (e.g., Albioma and Drax).
Since GreenVolt positioning lies in between traditional biomass players and pure
renewable players, it emerges with unique characteristics, such as, expertise on
biomass (market leader in Portugal), asset rotation (due to V-Ridium), and on
developing plants to RtB or COD.
As a result, we defined two peer groups, Peer Group 1 (Figure 26) and Peer
Group 2 (Figure 27), to better understand if competitors have the required
portfolio to capture market share. We considered firms with market cap between
€0.8bn-€2bn and present in Europe (an exception to this is EDP Renováveis, with
market cap above €20bn, because we deemed interesting to include a Portuguese
peer). Notwithstanding, we note key differences. For example, regarding Albioma,
half of its biomass installed capacity is overseas, and Ence and Drax are not pure
players (the former has a pulp business and the latter has other generation
sources) (Table 1). As for solar PV and wind players, we highlight the discrepancy
of installed capacity between GreenVolt and the other peers: GreenVolt has 45MW
(solar PV), whereas Audax, the peer with lowest market cap, has 180MW (solar
PV and wind) (Table 2).

Cost of Equity
We used the Capital Asset Pricing Model (“CAPM”) to compute the cost of equity.
The components used are risk free, market risk premium (“MRP”) and beta.
The proxy used as risk-free was the German Bund 10Y Yield as it has a low
default risk and the same currency as the GreenVolt’s cash flows. Even
though it is not the same duration of the cash flows, this yield is more tradable
than the Bund 30Y Yield, therefore the best proxy for the risk-free. As for MRP,
after carefully analysing the methodology, we used 6.0%, as suggest by KPMG.62
Despite, the best strategy to avoid systematic biases and noisiness is to use
monthly returns, GreenVolt’s stock only went public on 15 July of 2021, therefore
it would not be possible to have sixty data points for raw regressions, as a
result, we opted to choose weekly returns. Nevertheless, as GreenVolt stock is
traded on a continuous basis, we do not expect the beta to be highly impacted
between choosing weekly or monthly data. We graphed the rolling betas to search
for patterns in the stock’s risk (Figure 48).
To forecast the equity beta in the future, we used historical returns as we believe
they will remain stable over time. We estimated the raw beta of GreenVolt by
regressing its returns against the Morgan Stanley Capital International
(“MSCI”) World Index (proxy of market return). 63 It resulted in a beta of 0.63
with a confidence interval of [0.12, 1.14]. Given the broad range, and the fact that
individual firm betas can be profoundly influenced by unrepeatable events like
financial crises, we believe the best strategy to improve precision is to create
an industry beta. As a result, we estimated the raw betas of each peer groups
following the same technique abovementioned. Afterwards, we applied the
smoothing process used by Bloomberg, based on the evidence that betas
converge to 1 in the long term.

betas of different firms across an industry, we need to offset the effect of
leverage embedded by the financial risk. Considering Franco Modigliani and

the default risk in this industry is low, therefore we considered the beta of debt as

Profitability, Liquidity, and Capital Structure
GreenVolt gross margin ratio has been improving over the past four years, a
consequence of increasing revenues which offset the nominal rise in costs,
indicating a higher profitability (Figure 52). The decrease in 2021a of EBIT and
net income margin is related to the new debt contracted that year, which
consequently increased interest rates (Figure 53). GreenVolt profitability related to
sales is slightly above the peer groups. Looking at GreenVolt profitability related to
investments, Return on Assets (“ROA”) and Return on Total Capital peaked in
2020 at 0.09 and 0.17, respectively, both above peers. In 2021, it decreased to
0.01 and 0.04, respectively (Figure 54 and Figure 55). The low ROA is explained
by the fact that GreenVolt is an asset-heavy company and in a growth stage.
Therefore, its low ROA is acceptable.

significantly above the two peer groups with a currency ratio of 4.44 and a cash
ratio of 3.50. Both ratios are above 1, which shows GreenVolt healthy financial
position to repay its obligations. From 2020 to 2021, the improvement in both
ratios is mainly due to the increase of €19m in cash equivalents. For this reason,

Terminal Value
By 2044e, the growth rate is stabilized in all scenarios (i.e., steady state), and as
such we applied a perpetuity, for which we used the Gordon Growth Model to
calculate it. For biomass, looking at the base case, the level of NOPLAT used as
a terminal cash flow is based on a normalized level of revenues around 1%,
sustainable margins and Return on Invested Capital (“ROIC”) of c.5.8%, which
reflects the midpoint of the firm business cycle. Same rationale applies for DG.
In almost all scenarios ROIC is above WACC, which means GreenVolt is
creating value and it is expected to be sustained over time. For the biomass
segment, given its moderate/low ROIC, GreenVolt should focus on improving
returns before growing, while for distributed energy generation, given its high-
ROIC level, GreenVolt should focus on growth (Figure 60 and Figure 61).
Overall, the growth results from the product of the reinvestment rate (“RR”) and
ROIC after the firm reaches steady state in 2044e. All growth rates are below
the nominal GDP growth, therefore we consider it to be a fair value as the
Company will not be growing faster than the overall economy, as expected for this
industry. The negative growth rate in the bear scenario for the biomass segment
means the Company is shrinking and decaying in profit margins, which is partially
by the fact that we do not factor in FiT extensions, which demonstrates their
relevance for GreenVolt equity story.

Sensitivity Analysis
To assess the robustness of our model underlyings, we will analyse the
sensitiveness of each segment’s EV against some factors we believe to have the
most impact.
Biomass
First, we estimated the impact on EV from changing the segment’s WACC (5.71%)
and perpetuity growth rate (0.57%). Looking at Table 9, if we decrease WACC by
2pp and increase g by the same amount, EV increases by 5% (to c.€561m), and if
we increase WACC by 2pp and decrease g by the same amount, EV decreases
by 4% (to c.€510m). This means both variables are robust, since the variations
they yield on EV are not considerably impactful. Then, since this segment is subject
to some degree of uncertainty in the UK due to being subject to electricity market
prices, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to the average selling price, holding g
constant. As a starting point we have considered the lowest selling price forecasted
(£199.74/MWh) and as a last point, we have considered the maximum selling price
which coincides with the energy market cap (£285.61/MWh). In these extreme
cases, the EV would decrease to c.€395m and increase to c.€596m, implying a
downside and upside of 26% and 12%, respectively (Table 10). This high
sensitiveness indicates how crucial the average selling price is for
GreenVolt, especially in the UK, since they are market-tied.
Solar PV and wind
Assessing the impact on this segment’s EV by changing WACC (5.24%) and the
asset rotation strategy (75%), we conclude the percentage of assets to be sold at
RtB or COD is a resilient factor, allowing GreenVolt some headroom to decide
whether to farm down any given asset or to sell it. Regarding WACC, it depicts
again our high level of confidence on this factor: a decrease of 2pp holding the
asset rotation strategy level constant, leads to a small increase of 2% of the EV
(Table 11).

Recommendation
Although we recognise the outstanding performance of the Tilbury acquisition
in the UK (which is expected to benefit from sky-rocketing wholesale prices in the
upcoming years) and the inflation-indexed biomass business in Portugal, we
find that the geopolitical crisis in Europe could negatively impact the expected
returns from the asset rotation strategy, as buyers will face now greater
uncertainty regarding construction costs.
Notwithstanding, we believe in GreenVolt’s ability to support future growth. The
core biomass business presents high visibility of returns, which enjoy a long-
term fixed life. This, together with the Company’s expansion into solar PV and
wind in Europe, supports our equity growth story. Additionally, pursuing an asset
rotation strategy for most of projects may help to curb risks while allowing value
creation at an early stage, and to finance future growth. Regarding funding,
GreenVolt raised €100m early in July to prepare its balance sheet for daring
investments and to speed up its pipeline development. Moreover, in our view, the
Company has capacity to take on leverage to step up its investment plan, to which
high demand for green bonds is also favourable.
Nevertheless, GreenVolt’s equity story is largely based on early-stage
pipeline, to which a certain level of successful rate must be factored in (if the
Company were to successfully execute 100% of the asset rotation pipeline, we
would see c.42% upside), and on management dependency (CEO João Manso
Neto and V-Ridium’s deep expertise, being the former subject to a legal
investigation). Since we believe GreenVolt has the necessary tools to carry on its
ambitious asset developments, we view these potential downsides as limited.
We initiate coverage at Buy.
