November 2019

China’s airports are some of the biggest on the planet and handle well over a billion passenger
journeys every year. Beijing’s just-opened Daxing airport is the most expensive ever built, with plans
to handle 100 million passenger journeys a year. While those numbers are impressive, with the
number of travelers only likely to keep heading higher, China’s leading listed airports lag in one key
area compared to international peers: profitability. In this report, we take a deep dive into what really
drives profits and why we are confident that there is an earnings upcycle ahead.
On first glance, it can look challenging as all the large airports are built and operated by state-
owned enterprises and only a small portion of the sky is given over to civilian use which limits
the utilisation of runways. But in fact those close ties to the government give airports support in
the local areas they operate in, while a range of market-friendly reforms are being rolled out that
should boost profitability and returns.
For Shanghai International Airport (SIA) and Guangzhou Baiyun International Airport (GBIAC),
the country’s second and third biggest by passenger traffic, we see a rapid ramp-up in duty-free
business as the biggest earnings drivers. Perfumes and high-end cosmetics are especially
popular among travelers in China, which is largest sales category in duty-free shop, we see
duty-free sales contributing to four-fifths of profits for tier-1 airports by 2025e. But for Shenzhen
Airport (SACL) and Xiamen Airport (XIAC), they are raising the fees they charge airlines for
takeoffs and landings while also encouraging bigger planes and more international passengers
as they are both more profitable. Added to all this are new runways and terminals being built
and opened across China that are easing bottlenecks and boosting passenger numbers.
Elsewhere in this report:
 We analyze how higher investment returns will drive up long-term value for tier-1 airports
 We show why Chinese consumers are spending more at duty-free airport shops in China
 Why airports already offer high payout ratios and dividend yields with net margins and
ROEs both above the A-share average and close to those in the consumer sector
We initiate on SIA with a Buy as it is China’s most international and profitable airport, and also
initiate on XIAC with a Buy given its PE is far below both its peers and its historical average. We

Equities ● Airports

China’s aviation industry has grown rapidly over the past thirty years with the country now home
to the world’s second-biggest market in terms of passengers handled in 2018. Airports across
this vast country now number 235, from just 78 in 1978, and 37 of them handle over 10m
passengers a year. Eight of the world’s top 50 airports by passenger traffic are now in mainland
China1.
As we show in this report, airports are now a lot more than just a place to catch a ride as they
are becoming shopping destinations in themselves, especially duty-free shopping. Secondly,
while China has plenty of small and medium-sized airports, the majority of these operate at a
loss and it’s the larger airports that are the most profitable and the focus of this report. In 2018,
the six listed airports of Shanghai, Beijing, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Xiamen and Haikou
collectively contributed 32% to the industry’s total revenue and 59% to the total profit.
For tier-1 airports (those located in tier-1 cities), while passenger traffic is growing steadily, the
really exciting earnings drivers are rising spending by travelers and better monetisation of their
land, buildings and equipment. We see airports raising their aeronautical charges, like landing
and parking fees, to be in line with international peers and adopting more market-pricing
policies. For airports like SIA, the biggest growth should come from its shift to handling more
international travelers and higher spending in duty free. It’s no surprise then that overall, we see
earnings and investment returns of tier-1 airports currently in an upcycle.

Big airports to fly high
Over half a billion passengers each year. An airport with plans for eight
runways. Some of the cheapest duty free prices in the world. Like many
industries in China, the country’s airports have some dazzling statistics.
But it’s easy to lose sight of what the real drivers of profit are for the
large listed airports and which is the focus of this report. In short, we see
an earnings upcycle ahead thanks a wide range of positives like
increasing numbers of international travelers, higher spending on
cosmetics at duty free and higher charges for takeoffs and landings.

In our view, tier-1 airports are favoured by investors for these four reasons:
 Trips per capita, a key measure for the aviation industry, is well below international peers at
just 0.4 in China compared to two in Europe and the US. Airbus expects a CAGR of 7.3%
for RPK (revenue passenger kilometer) of domestic routes in China and an even higher rate
for international routes over 2017-2027, indicating long-term sustainable growth ahead for
the airport industry.
 In China, major airports are constructed and operated by state-owned enterprises, which
enables tier-1 airports to enjoy a natural regional monopoly.
 Given high passenger traffic as well as a high utilisation rate, tier-1 airports have strong
economies of scale and are well positioned to improve passenger consumption, which
should lift their profit margins and ROEs.
 For tier-1 airports, their changing business models can help generate superior cash flow,
and their CROIC (adjusted free cash flow/invested capital) is well above the A-share
average and close to that in the consumer sector. Airports generally offer higher payout
ratios and dividend yields.

Industry outlook
Passenger throughput, which counts each inbound and outbound journey separately, at airports
in China rose from 406m in 2008 to 1.27bn in 2018 (12% CAGR), accompanied by air traffic
movements (equivalent to a takeoff or landing) rising from 4.2m to 11.1m over the same period
(10% CAGR). In 1H19, the y-o-y growth in air passenger throughput and air traffic movements
moderated to 8.1% and 6.3% for the whole airport industry, respectively, while those numbers
fell to just 3.1% and 0.73% for large tier-1 airports. This was due to the slowing economy and
the Civil Aviation Administration of China’s (CAAC) stricter controls over the volume of flights.
However, in our view, tier-1 airports will benefit from accelerating growth in passenger traffic as
they add capacity over the next three years. We estimate passenger throughput growth at tier-1
airports will recover to 3.8% in 2019e, 7.1% in 2020e and 7.6% in 2021e. We see earnings
being driven by:

SIA (600009 CH, RMB77.68; Buy; TP: RMB92.87)
Positioned as an international hub for passengers and an Asia-Pacific freight transportation
center, Shanghai Pudong International Airport (SIA) is the ninth-largest passenger airport and
the third largest cargo airport in the world. In mainland China, it is the second-largest passenger
airport and the biggest cargo airport. Over 2013-2018, SIA recorded a CAGR of 9.4% in
passenger throughput, 12.3% in revenue and 17% in net profit.
In addition to its leading cost advantages and efficient management, SIA plans to eventually
have eight runways and five terminals, which would offer it the potential to become a “super
airport”. With satellite terminals starting operations in 2019, the company has doubled its
terminal capacity. Under its new duty-free contracts, the company is likely to register notable
growth in duty-free revenue and earnings. According to our estimates, SIA’s duty-free net profit
and cash earnings will collectively exceed RMB38bn over 2019-25e, and advertisement
earnings will double as its advertisement contracts are renewed from 2021 onwards.
We estimate that SIA can achieve a CAGR of 18.3% in revenue and 19.0% in net profit
attributable to parent company over 2019-2021e. We arrive at a target price of RMB92.87
based on our two-stage DCF model, implying 20% upside. We initiate coverage with a Buy
rating. The market consensus on 3Q19 and 2Q19 earnings points to a 29.8x and a 30.2x 2019e
PE respectively, while our earnings estimates imply a 27.3x 2019e PE, more than 2SD above its
historical average. In our view, this reflects the robust growth of the company’s duty-free
business and continued decline in its discount rate, so the current valuation is attractive in our
view. As an industry leader, we believe SIA, among our covered companies, demonstrates the
best internationalisation, profitability and investment returns, and is able to generate strong free
cash flow. SIA is our preferred stock for long-term investment. Share price catalysts: a marked
increase in air traffic movements and sales of duty-free goods beating our expectations.
XIAC (600897 CH, RMB21.11; Buy; TP: RMB24.86)
Xiamen International Airport Co Ltd (XIAC) is a typical tier-2 airport in China but with a competitive
valuation. With one runway and two terminals, XIAC reported a CAGR of 6.1% in passenger
throughput, 7.2% in revenue and 2.8% in net profit over 2013-18. Its shareholder returns are decent
given a payout ratio of 75% and a dividend yield of more than 4% since 2016.
To address its capacity bottleneck created by having just one runway, since 2017, XIAC has
increased the takeoffs and landings of wide-body aircrafts that carry more passengers than
regular aircraft, bolstering passenger traffic. In our view, air travel demand will stay robust in
Xiamen as it is an important tourist city in China, while the company’s unit aeronautical revenue
should continue to rise. At present, the brand new Xiamen Xiang’an Airport is being constructed
by XIAC’s controlling shareholder, and we expect the construction to be ready for operation

GBIAC (600004 CH, RMB18.50; Hold; TP: RMB20.63)
Guangzhou Baiyun International Airport Co Ltd (GBIAC) is the third-largest airport in mainland
China. From 2013-2018, GBIAC recorded a CAGR of 5.9% for its passenger throughput, 8.5%
for revenue and 4.1% for net profit. GBIAC focuses on investor returns, with its cash dividend
payout ratio averaging 41.4% since its IPO in April 2003.
After T2 operations started in 2018, its capacity was boosted while the high growth in
international routes has buoyed its aeronautical profit given the higher fees. In our view,
GBIAC’s non-aeronautical business has the greatest growth potential given GBIAC’s duty-free
business is expanding rapidly and catching up with SIA and BCIA. We expect GBIAC’s duty-free
business to deliver a 35% CAGR in franchise and rental revenue over 2018-2021e. With a focus
on keeping costs under control, the company has quit some non-core businesses since 2018
and instead increased its labour outsourcing.
Based on our earnings estimates, GBIAC shares currently trade at 46.8x 2019e PE, largely
consistent with Wind consensus (50x 2019e PE) and more than 2SD above its historical
average. In our view, the current valuation is fair. We believe this is simply a result of a passive
rerating caused by the significant reduction in short-term earnings due to its regular refund from
the Civil Aviation Development Fund being cancelled, which was essentially a type of
government subsidy support. On the other hand, a notable increase in both the commission rate
and fixed minimum rent under the new duty-free contracts has substantially driven up the
company’s profitability and valuations. Looking ahead, the company’s earnings may exceed our
expectations given its improving operating efficiency and market competitive power.
SACL (000089 CH, RMB9.71; Hold; TP: RMB9.24)
As one of the six tier-1 airports in China, Shenzhen Airport Co Ltd (SACL) is an international airline
hub and set to benefit from the Greater Bay Area policies and Shenzhen’s new role as the country’s
pilot demonstration area that will help promote the finance and tech sectors. Over 2013-18, SACL
reported a CAGR of 8.9% in passenger throughput, 7.2% in revenue and 5.9% in net profit.
Though SCAL is less international than tier-1 peers, it has accelerated its expansion of
international routes and encouraged takeoffs and landings of wide-body aircrafts by providing
subsidies and preferential policies. This should improve the company’s aeronautical revenue
mix in the long run. SACL’s duty-free business is still small given it only started developing it far
later than others. In our view, the operation of satellite terminals and the renewal of duty-free
contracts around 2021 will provide SACL an opportunity to grow rapidly. The investment return
on SACL’s new capacity is currently low but we expect the IRR of the satellite terminal
expansion project to reach 7.9%, higher than that of the T3 expansion project (4.7%) and the
company’s capital cost (7.3%). This suggests that the company’s value should grow in the
future, albeit slowly.
Based on our earnings estimates, SACL shares currently trade at a 29.7x 2019e PE, c1SD
above their historical average. In our view, the current valuation is fair given Shenzhen’s
favourable location.

Where we are different
SIA: We believe the market overlooks SIA’s role as a global freight transportation center and
the long-term strategic boost this provides to the utilisation rate of its runways. We recognise
though that the global economic slowdown may dampen cargo earnings in the short term.
Meanwhile, the market may be underestimating the increase in short-term costs after the
satellite terminals are put into operation. The market may also not fully anticipate a scenario

Becoming more international with passengers spending more
Globally, passenger throughput and air traffic movements of well-developed tier-1 airports are
stabilizing at an average 50m-70m and 0.4m-0.8m, respectively. In 2018, the four tier-1 airports
in China, namely BCIA, SIA, GBIAC and SACL, recorded passenger throughput of 101m, 74m,
70m and 49m, and air traffic movements of 0.61m, 0.50m, 0.70m and 0.49m, respectively.
These have reached levels seen at other tier-1 peers in the world. But with passenger traffic
growth now leveling off, the earnings drivers have shifted from the rapid throughput growth to
improving passenger consumptions and better monetisation of their land, buildings and
equipment, aka improving the resource value of airports.
Due to high fixed costs and operation leverage of the airport industry, the long-term valuations
of airports are determined by the fees they charge, asset utilisation rates and their resource
value. We believe that tier-1 airports in China are currently in the process of internationalisation
and have started to see increasing passenger consumption. Relative to international peers, we
see significant upside for aeronautical charge standards, runway utilisation rates and non-
aeronautical revenue of domestic airports.

What’s driving up aeronautical earnings?
In China, charges collected by airports are classified into aeronautical charges (e.g. takeoffs
and landing charges, passenger service charges, and security service charges) and non-
aeronautical charges (e.g. the leasing of check-in counters and lounges, handling and logistics
services, etc.). Aeronautical charges are mainly regulated by the government with the CAAC
raising aeronautical charges five times between 1992 and 2017. After 2007, the CAAC has
gradually relaxed its control over non-aeronautical charges and allowed the market to play a
greater role, leading to increases in these charges.
We don’t see any changes to the overall trend of increasing charge standards and more market-
related reforms that started. In 2017, Charges for typical narrow-body aircrafts (i.e. B737-800)
and typical wide-body aircrafts (i.e. A330-300) on domestic routes at tier-1 airports have risen
by 13-14% and 8-9%, respectively. As non-aeronautical charges are already largely market-
driven, the reforms to charges are concentrated on the aeronautical business where we expect
charges to be raised every 5-10 years.

Improving runway utilisation rate is key to a high asset turnover ratio
For airports, their capacity utilisation is mainly determined by their runways and terminals. Most
of the time, the utilisation rate of terminals at domestic tier-1 airports exceeds 100% (meaning
more passengers travel through the terminals than what the terminals were designed), on par
with international standards. Given terminals were designed for fairly conservative estimates of
passengers, we think the terminal utilisation rate can stay above 130-140% in the long term.
Despite the potential short-term adverse impact of airport expansion on the utilisation of
terminals, the enormous air travel demand in China would keep the utilisation rate of terminals
at a high level in the long term.
However, China’s tier-1 airports (BCIA excluded) generally handle fewer landings and takeoffs
per runway than their international peers. We think the airspace bottleneck is the main reason
for the low amount of landings and takeoffs despite a runway utilisation rate (number of
movements in the busy periods/movement capacity per hour for a single runway) that is higher
than 90%. In China, only 20%-25% of the airspace is for civilian use with the rest reserved for
the military, while this figure is above 80% in the US. We believe civilian airspace is unlikely to
be expanded or there to be significant increase in routes in the short run. However, as the air
traffic control system reform advances, we believe there is potentially progress in terms of
civilian airspace expanded and routes increased at airports in busy regions like the Pearl River
Delta region and Shanghai. This could shorten the ground holding period for aircrafts, and lift
the runway utilisation and asset turnover ratio for airports.

Going international could optimise the revenue mix
The degree of internationalisation is critical to an airport’s revenue and earnings mix. Highly
profitable airports around the world, such as London Heathrow Airport, Hong Kong International
Airport, Incheon International Airport and Los Angeles International Airport, are all key
international airline hubs. At China’s airports, aeronautical charges for international routes are

narrow-body ones. Therefore, we believe increasing the internationalisation of airports will
improve the aeronautical revenue mix for airports and boost growth of the highly profitable non-
aeronautical businesses like duty-free stores.
We believe the two airports in Shanghai (SIA and Hongqiao International) are comparable to
airports in New York and London metropolitan regions, in terms of internationalisation2.

passengers as a percentage of total passengers stabilised at 35%-40% for airports in New York
and London metropolitan regions, and were close to 30% for Los Angeles International Airport.

airports in the three tier-1 cities (Beijing, Shanghai and Guangzhou) in the future, with SACL

The impact from downtown duty-free stores
Expectations are high for policies that will allow for more downtown duty-free stores aimed at
outbound Chinese consumers. While such moves could attract consumers and away from
airport shopping we still believe airport duty-free stores are the most convenient and efficient
shopping channel to travellers. For duty-free operators, expanding airport duty-free stores is
crucial to expanding their business and building up their brand reputation. We expect downtown
duty-free stores to divert c15% of sales away from airports, which is manageable, in our view.
Below is our analysis on the impact of such diversion across three potential stages.
Stage 1: Downtown duty-free stores are categorised into downtown inbound duty-free stores
and downtown outbound duty-free stores, and we believe the biggest business opportunity lies
with Chinese consumers. At present, downtown inbound duty-free stores are only open to
Chinese consumers, who can buy goods there up to 180 days after they travelled abroad.
These stores though are suffering from sluggish growth as they are run by China National
Service Corporation for Chinese Personnel Working Abroad (CNSC) and China Travel Service
(HK) Group Corporation who have limited experience in running retail stores. Downtown
outbound duty-free stores are only open to non-Chinese travelers who buy there and then pick
up their goods at the airport.
Stage 2: If policies were introduced that opened up downtown duty-free stores to outbound
Chinese passengers then it is likely that the size of these stores, product categories and brands,
and product types would all improve and boost their appeal to consumers. Currently, the area of
duty-free stores in the airports exceeds 15,000 square metres at SIA and BCIA each, and 6,000
square meters at GBIAC, with a wide variety of products on offer. By contrast, downtown duty-
free stores are far smaller and barely challenge the dominance of their airport-based
counterparts.
Stage 3: We believe downtown duty-free stores could impact the duty-free industry if they
expanded quickly and were well operated. On one hand, the rise of downtown duty-free stores
would lift the penetration rate of the duty-free market, expand the duty-free market size and
generate new demand. On the other hand, this will inevitably divert certain sales away from
airport duty-free stores. From the perspective of duty-free store operators, the consumer base of
airport duty-free stores is different from that of downtown duty-free stores, so by focusing on this
incremental demand would help them maximise profits. From a consumers’ perspective,
however, airport duty-free stores remain the most convenient and efficient shopping channel.
Therefore, in the most likely scenario where duty-free store operators focus on exploring
incremental demand, the potential impact to airport duty-free stores should be manageable, and

Rising IRR of new capacity would increase long-term value
One major issue for airports is that they face capacity bottlenecks. At tier-1 airports, while
passenger flow growth has moderated, we expect it to stay above 5% over the medium term
and airports are still expanding. However, whether the investment return (IRR) of new capacity
like runways and terminals can exceed the capital cost (WACC) and continue to increase will
determine the long-term value of airports. Judging from the market performance of listed
airports, changes in their market capitalisation are highly correlated to their investment
expenditure and returns.
SIA’s much higher investment in S1 & S2 satellite terminals and its expected IRR which are
higher than that of T2 terminal have driven up SIA’s market capitalisation by more than 200%
from the end of 2016, and it now ranks as the largest airport in China by market capitalisation.
Elsewhere, we expect the IRR of GBIAC’s T2 project will be higher than that of T1 terminal,
though the airport’s market capitalisation has increased only by c150% since 2016 due to
modest growth in investment and the impact from the cancellation of its refund from the Civil
Aviation Development Fund. With regard to SACL, we expect the IRR of its satellite terminal
project to be much higher than that of its T3 project. However, we believe that there is limited
room for the company to increase its value given its IRR is similar to its capital costs, explaining
its tepid 30% growth in market capitalisation since the end of 2016.
The unit construction costs of tier-1 airports are all at similar levels, so the IRR of expansion
projects mainly relies on the contribution of international businesses and the non-aeronautical

Next growth driver: Satellite terminal and new duty-free contract
Terminal capacity to double
The company currently operates four runways. A fifth runway is being built by the group and is
currently used for China commercial aircraft flight testing. In the future, it will be used by SIA for
civil aviation, providing the company with ample capacity in terms of runways. While the airport
can handle just 42m passengers in T1 and T2, the actual capacity utilisation was as high as
176% in 2018, leading to bottlenecks and meaning the terminals have limited room for further
passenger throughput.
In September 2019, terminal design throughput increased to 80m passengers after SIA’s
satellite terminal began operations. According to Civil Aviation Administration policy,
adjustments to runway movement’s capacity per hour is only permitted if the on-time rate of
flight operations of airports and the on-time rate of flight operations for the first flight of the day
are above 80% in at least nine months of the year. As of June 2019, SIA has met this
requirement. We note also that in July 2019 the Civil Aviation Administration and the Shanghai
municipal government have entered into an agreement to expand aviation airspace and
increase flight slots capacity and supply for the two major airports in Shanghai. We think the
company’s capacity will be increased from 2021 to 2023, accelerating the growth of both its
aeronautical business and non-aeronautical business.

Connected rental cost under control
Property leases between SIA and its controlling shareholders include the second and the third
runways, terminal T2, the land for the satellite terminal, the fourth runway and its land, apron leases
and office spaces. The relevant rent is one of the company’s main costs, amounting to RMB730m in
2018, and representing 16.2% of operating costs. The company expects rental costs to increase to
RMB1,048m in 2019. Since 2011, the company’s associated lease expenses have surged 380% and
the average rental has increased from RMB34/square metre in 2017 to RMB66/square metres in
2019e while the market worries that rental costs could rise further.
However, according to our estimates, as of 2H 2018, Shanghai Airport Group’s (上海机场集团)
SIA related assets and land were worth less than RMB17.19bn. The rental yield appears
reasonable at 6%. According to industry practice, the airport land is divided into security land (保
障性用地) and commercial land. The security land (保障性用地) mainly includes supporting
facilities in the flight area (excluding the runway). The rental is based on the costs, often at
RMB5-10/square metre/year; the rental of commercial land is based on market prices. SIA’s unit
rental level is the highest among listed airports meaning room for further increases is limited.

been far lower than those of SIA and BCIA. In 2018, the company’s per capita duty free sales
were RMB53, just 17% of SIA’s. We think this can be attributed to these three reasons:
1)
GBIAC’s duty free areas are small with few brands, categories, and product models,
creating a poor customer experience which hinders spending. International inbound
passenger traffic has not been monetised due to the absence of an arrival duty free
business.
2)
Product prices of the departure duty-free shops operated by Guangzhou Xinmian (广州新
免公司) under China Duty Free Group are higher compared with the same products in
Sunrise duty-free shops at SIA, weighing on consumer demand.
3)
The average income level of local residents in Guangdong Province was lower than that of
Beijing and Shanghai. The share of European and American routes at GBIAC is
significantly lower compared to SIA and BCIA, so the spending power of the company’s
international route passengers is lower than the levels in Shanghai and Beijing.
However, the company is now catching up in the duty free business, especially when compared
to the airports in Shanghai and Beijing. We estimate per capita duty-free consumption at GBIAC
will climb to RMB100 by around 2022e due to:
 Firstly, with the launch of T2 and arrival duty-free shops in T1 since 2018, GBIAC’s duty-
free business area has substantially jumped, from 1,400 square metres to 6,044 square
metres today. The brands, categories and size of duty-free shops have increased while the
duty-free rents are rising. The shopping experience for customers and their willingness to
spend have improved.
 Secondly, given CDF acquired Sunrise Duty-free Company (Shanghai) in 2018, we think
CDF will consolidate the supplier channel; this means prices of the same kinds of duty-free
products at GBIAC should move closer to those of SIA.
 Thirdly, the company has been developing international routes with plans to open 30
international routes in the next three years, of which 14 are European and American routes.
The increase in international routes is conducive to lifting the spending power of
passengers.
The company started operating the new duty free contract in H2 2018. Both fixed minimum
rents and commission rates have substantially increased as the profitability of the duty free
business has surged.

Changing the aeronautical business structure
Incentive to use wide-body aircrafts
In 2018, the company spent cRMB60m on encouraging airlines to use wide-body aircrafts given
aeronautical charges are generally c1.9x that of narrow-body aircraft, so more wide-body
aircrafts should generate higher aeronautical charges for SACL. Wide-body aircrafts accounted
for 11-12% in 2018.
Accelerated internationalisation
In 2007, CAAC positioned BCIA, SIA and GBIAC as international hub airports while SACL was
the regional trunk airport. Fast forward to 2016, and China’s 13th Five-Year Plan first positioned
SACL as an international hub, with CAAC proposing to strengthen its international hub function
in 2017. Currently, the government and company offer route subsidies and movement discounts
for about three years to airlines who launch their first international routes at SACL.
Driven by government policies and company discounts, the internationalisation of SACL has
been developing rapidly in recent years. The annual growth of international and regional
passenger throughput is steady at over 25%. In 2018, the share of international passengers
amounted to 9.3%, but there is still big difference when compared with the 25-35% level in
Beijing, Shanghai and Guangzhou. As the unit aeronautical charges for the company’s
international and regional routes are generally c1.6x that of domestic routes, we believe the
rising share of international and regional passengers will help grow overall aeronautical charges
for SACL.

Non-aeronautical business an opportunity

Among Tier-1 airports, the profitability of the company’s advertising business is relatively high.

Duty free business to be improved, capacity expansion an opportunity
SACL is the latest airport to start its duty free business among Tier-1 airports. It opened
outbound duty-free shops and inbound duty-free shops in terminal T3 in 2013 and 2017,
respectively. All operators are state-owned duty-free goods companies in Shenzhen. We
estimate the duty-free shops of SACL achieved sales of cRMB150m, with per capita duty-free
consumption of RMB33 in 2018. There is a big difference between the company’s duty-free
business and other Tier-1 airports’. In our view, the reasons are as follows:
 Weak operational capabilities of duty-free shops at SACL. The attraction of airport duty-
free shops is the selection of high-quality goods and low selling prices. There is a significant
difference between the duty-free shop at SACL and other operators of Tier-1 airports like
CDF and its subsidiary Sunrise Duty Free.
 Small duty-free shops are not attractive enough. Compared with BCIA, SIA and GBIAC,
the area of the duty-free shop at SACL is still relatively small at just 2,400 square metres.
However, we believe there is significant room for operational efficiency and higher rents at its
duty-free shops, based on:
 Firstly, the outbound and inbound duty-free contracts for SACL will expire in November 2020
and August 2022, respectively. The Airport will conduct a tender with more quality operators
likely to be introduced which could enhance the competitiveness of the duty-free shop of SACL
and boost duty-free consumption. Besides, compared with other Tier-1 airports, there is
significant upside in the fixed minimum rent and commission rate for SACL.
 Secondly, we think after the satellite terminal starts operations in 2021, the area of duty-free
shops could increase by more than 1,000 square metres. In the medium term, after the new
T4 commences operations and the duty-free shop expands further, product categories will
become more diversified.
 Thirdly, considering the runway capacity saturation at Hong Kong Airport and that the new
Hong Kong runway can only be launched after 2023, along with the improved international
routes layout of SACL, we think international passengers in Guangdong will gradually return
from Hong Kong, speeding up growth in the company’s duty free sales.

Slow revenue and earnings growth
Gaoqi Airport handled 26.55 million passengers and 192,000 air traffic movements in 2018,
indicating a CAGR of 6.1% and 2.8% over 2013-18, respectively. In the same year, the
company reported revenue of RMB1,777m and net profit attributable to the parent of
RMB506m, with 2013-18 CAGR at 7.2% and 2.8%, respectively.

Continued revenue mix and cost optimisation
Gaoqi Airport is currently in the third stage of its development.
 Stage 1 (from 2010 to 2014): Both passenger throughput and air traffic movements at
Gaoqi Airport rose quickly, thanks to ample capacity and strong air travel demand in
Xiamen. It also maintained double-digit growth in both revenue and earnings.
 Stage 2 (from 2014 to 2016): Terminal 4 was put in to use at the end of 2014, pushing the
passenger terminal utilisation rate down to 77% from 139%. However, there was a
slowdown in the growth of passenger throughput as the landing and takeoff movement
capacity per hour for its single runway had hit the upper limit, while the airport’s terminal
capacity had increased. The company’s revenue growth rate fell back to c5%, along with
short-term earning fluctuations due to the operation of Terminal 4.
 Stage 3 (Since 2017): Gaoqi Airport has been increasing the landings and takeoffs of wide-
body aircrafts to ease the bottleneck pressure on its runway capacity, driving up traffic
volume growth and revenue growth to 5-10% levels. In addition, Gaoqi Airport has also
been cutting down on costs to recover the profit margin and accelerate earnings growth.

Our ratings are re-calibrated against these bands at the time of any 'material change' (initiation or resumption of coverage, change
in target price or estimates).
Upside/Downside is the percentage difference between the target price and the share price.
Prior to this date, HSBC’s rating structure was applied on the following basis:
For each stock we set a required rate of return calculated from the cost of equity for that stock’s domestic or, as appropriate,
regional market established by our strategy team. The target price for a stock represented the value the analyst expected the
stock to reach over our performance horizon. The performance horizon was 12 months. For a stock to be classified as Overweight,
the potential return, which equals the percentage difference between the current share price and the target price, including the
forecast dividend yield when indicated, had to exceed the required return by at least 5 percentage points over the succeeding 12
months (or 10 percentage points for a stock classified as Volatile*). For a stock to be classified as Underweight, the stock was
expected to underperform its required return by at least 5 percentage points over the succeeding 12 months (or 10 percentage
points for a stock classified as Volatile*). Stocks between these bands were classified as Neutral.
*A stock was classified as volatile if its historical volatility had exceeded 40%, if the stock had been listed for less than 12 months
(unless it was in an industry or sector where volatility is low) or if the analyst expected significant volatility. However, stocks which
we did not consider volatile may in fact also have behaved in such a way. Historical volatility was defined as the past month's
average of the daily 365-day moving average volatilities. In order to avoid misleadingly frequent changes in rating, however,
volatility had to move 2.5 percentage points past the 40% benchmark in either direction for a stock's status to change.
